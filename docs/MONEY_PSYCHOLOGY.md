# Why Svinkki talks the way it does: money psychology behind the coach

Written 14 Nov 2026. What research on money psychology and behavioural finance says about talking to people
about money so that they act, how the coach's instructions use it, and references for the pitch.

How the references were checked: each was looked up on 14 Nov. **Checked** means the claim was read on the
publisher's, the university's or the authors' own page (often the abstract). **Abstract only** means only an
abstract or a search summary was seen: fine to name the study, not to quote its numbers. Nothing here was
tested on Svinkki's own users; it is the design basis, not a result.

## The idea in one paragraph

People act on money when it feels close, concrete and theirs, when the first step is small and easy, and when
they are not judged. Svinkki therefore starts from the customer's own figure, shows what a small change
becomes in euros over a month, a year and two years (worked out by code, never guessed), ties it to something
they want, offers one small step with a moment to do it ("on payday, move €10"), and leaves the choice with
them. When someone sounds stressed it says less, not more.

## Principles and what the coach does with them

| Principle | What the research says | What Svinkki does |
|---|---|---|
| Make the future concrete | People who see a vivid, personal future self put more aside, at least in experiments with hypothetical allocations (Hershfield et al. 2011). Distant things are thought of abstractly and motivate less (Trope & Liberman 2010). | Turns a change into euros over time: "€10 a month is €120 in a year and €240 in two", and ties it to the customer's own goal ("your trip to Japan"). The figures come from code. |
| Small first steps that grow | Save More Tomorrow: a small start that rises over time lifted the saving rate from 3.5% to 13.6% in 40 months at the first firm (Thaler & Benartzi 2004). | Suggests one small amount the customer's month can carry, never a big target first. |
| A plan with a moment | "When X happens, I do Y" plans raise goal attainment (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006; later estimates are smaller). New starts (a new month, payday) help (Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014). | Ends with one step and when to take it: "on payday on 15 October, move €10". |
| Defaults and one tap | Automatic enrolment raised participation, and people kept the default (Madrian & Shea 2001). | Offers to prepare the move for one-tap approval; it can be cancelled or changed any time. |
| Show progress | People speed up as a goal gets closer, even when the progress is partly given (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006). | Says what is already done ("€400 of €3,000 already set aside"). |
| Name the money | People treat money in labelled pots differently (Thaler 1999); earmarking for a goal helped low-income savers (Soman & Cheema 2011). | Uses the customer's own goal and pot names. |
| Gain, not fear | How a choice is framed changes it (Kahneman & Tversky 1979); in field reminders gain and loss wording did about equally well (Karlan et al. 2016). | Talks about what the customer keeps, never scare tactics. |
| No shame | People who expect to be judged hide debt and avoid help (Goode & Moorhouse 2023). People look away from their finances when the news may be bad (Karlsson, Loewenstein & Seppi 2009). | Never "should", "too much", "wasteful". Normalises ("a lot of people feel this before payday"). Opens with what is going right when there is something. |
| Less when stressed | Money worries take up mental bandwidth (Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao 2013). | A worried customer gets one human sentence, one number and one step. Debt trouble: points to Takuusäätiö's free advice. |
| Goals first, the customer decides | Coaching that follows the client's own goals improved financial wellbeing in a randomised evaluation (CFPB 2016). Autonomy matters in motivational interviewing (evidence mostly from health). | Asks what the money is for when it matters, offers a choice and ends with "your call". |
| Reminders tied to a goal | Reminders raise saving, best when tied to the saver's own goal (Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan & Zinman 2016). | Check-ins and suggestions name the customer's own goal and amount. |

## What is not claimed

- No study found that euros persuade better than percentages; that is an inference from construal-level
  theory.
- The Hershfield results are about hypothetical allocations, not money actually saved.
- Motivational interviewing evidence comes from health care, not banking.
- None of this has been measured on Svinkki's users.
